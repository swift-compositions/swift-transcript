# swift-transcript

Use this package when speech in a recording must become words with times, or when a text that was read aloud must be matched to what was heard, word by word, to time captions or to choose between takes. `Transcript` is pure Swift with no platform framework: a `Transcript` is the spoken words in order, each a `Transcript.Word` with its text, its span in exact seconds (`Range<Rational>` of swift-rational, the same seconds swift-timeline counts in) and the listener's confidence where it gives one (`Interval.Unit<Double>` of swift-interval). A `Transcript.Script` is the known text, its words in paragraphs and lines. `Transcript.Alignment(_:to:)` sets the script against the transcript: for each script word whether it was heard, as what and when, and each spoken word the script does not hold, with the script word it follows. `pace` (a `Rate` of swift-rate, words a second) and `pauses(longerThan:)` measure a transcript, and `transcript(ofParagraph:)` gives the part of one paragraph to measure. `Transcript Dictation` hears a recording on the device with the system's dictation, from a path or from a `Signal<Float>` of swift-sound, `Transcript(dictating:in:expecting:)`, where the Speech framework is, and needs the `Dictation` trait, which brings swift-sound (with its `Decoding` trait), swift-path and swift-rfc-5646 in; without it the library is empty. Any other listener (a Whisper model, a human transcriber) is used by building the `Transcript` from its words.

The module is `Transcript`, not `Speech`, because a module named `Speech` hides Apple's Speech framework from every target in the same build.

## Laws

- A transcript keeps its words in the order given. Its `span` runs from the earliest start of a word to the latest end, and is `nil` with no words.
- A word's key is what counts when it is compared: its letters and digits, lowercased, with the accents taken off Latin vowels, ñ and ç (so a stress accent such as in "één" or "dát" does not count), and nothing else. Punctuation, quotes of every kind (‘ ’ „ ” « »), dashes, apostrophes and hyphens fall away. Numbers compare by their digits: "1964" matches "1964", and a number written in words against one in digits is a missing word and an extra one.
- A script's paragraphs end at a line with nothing but whitespace, and its words are runs of characters between whitespace. A word whose key is empty, such as a lone dash, is not a word of the script, and a paragraph or a line with no word is not counted. Paragraphs count from 0 over the script, lines from 0 within their paragraph.
- The alignment is the one of least cost over the whole script. Likeness is one less the edit distance between two keys over the longer one's characters. A script word and a spoken word with the same key cost nothing and are exact; more than half alike, they cost one less their likeness and are a slip. A script word not heard costs 1, and so does a spoken word not in the script, so two words less than half alike are a missing word and an extra one. Two script words heard as one spoken word ("over het" as "overhet"), or one heard as two ("zonnebloemen" as "zonne bloemen"), cost 0.8 plus one less the likeness of the joined keys, and are taken only when the joined keys are more than nine tenths alike.
- Where two alignments cost the same, a script word is matched to the later spoken word, so a phrase read twice is timed by its last reading and the first reading is extra.
- A heard script word spans its spoken word. A script word heard as two spoken words spans from the start of the first to the end of the second. Two script words heard as one spoken word divide its span in proportion to the characters of their keys, exactly.
- An extra spoken word follows the script word before it in the alignment, or none when it comes before the first. A paragraph's transcript holds the spoken words its script words were heard as and the extras that follow one of its words, each once, in the order spoken.
- `pace` is the number of words over the length of the span, exactly, and is `nil` with no words or a span of no length. `pauses(longerThan:)` are the silences from one word's end to the next word's start, in the order spoken, longer than the given seconds; words that overlap make no pause.
- `Transcript(dictating:in:expecting:)` of a path decodes the recording with swift-sound at 16,000 ticks a second and hears that sound; a recording that cannot be decoded fails with `.unreadable`.
- `Transcript(dictating:in:expecting:)` of a sound hears it on the device with the system's dictation (`DictationTranscriber` with `SpeechAnalyzer`), with nothing sent off it, its channels mixed down into the format the analyzer asks for; the sound must be at the analyzer's rate, 16,000 ticks a second on macOS 27.2, or it fails with `.unreadable`; the first call for a language downloads its assets through AssetInventory. The language is required, and `expecting` are handed to the analyzer as contextual strings, as given. A language the device has no dictation for fails with `.language`, and a piece the dictation cannot hear with `.unreadable`.
- The sound is heard in pieces, each by an analyzer of its own, and each piece's words are put back at their time in the sound. `Transcript.Dictation.pieces(of:)` cuts the sound in the middle of each of its silences under −40 dBFS longer than a quarter of a second (swift-sound's `silences(below:longerThan:)`) and joins neighbouring stretches while a piece stays within `Transcript.Dictation.longest`, 9 seconds; a stretch with no such silence for longer stays one piece. The pieces are in order and together the whole sound. The reason is measured on macOS 27.2 with nl-NL: heard whole, a minute of read Dutch inside a longer recording came back with no words and no error, and a 14-second piece of it failed with `RecogFailed`; over 50 pieces of up to 9 seconds cut in silences, from two readings of about three minutes, every one was heard.
- A word is the dictation's runs joined up to whitespace, spanning from its first run's start to its last run's end, as sure as its least sure run, and its text is as the dictation wrote it; dictation writes no punctuation and writes numbers in digits.
- On-device dictation asked for no speech-recognition permission when run by `swift test` and a command-line tool on macOS 27.2, from the shell of a desktop app that may hold permissions of its own; a process elsewhere may be asked.
